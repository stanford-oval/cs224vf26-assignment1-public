# Clinical Identification and Phenotypic Spectrum of FBXO11-Related IDDFBA (OMIM #618089)

## Introduction and Diagnostic Challenges of FBXO11-Related IDDFBA

Intellectual developmental disorder with dysmorphic facies and behavioral abnormalities (IDDFBA; OMIM #618089) is an autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental syndrome caused by heterozygous pathogenic variants in the *FBXO11* gene (F1). Identifying affected individuals on purely phenotypic grounds is notoriously challenging in general pediatric and genetics clinics. Because the facial dysmorphisms are frequently subtle or nonspecific and the behavioral manifestations display variable penetrance, distinguishing *FBXO11*-related disorder from broad cohorts of syndromic intellectual disability requires careful evaluation of both core positive features and exclusionary negative signs (F1, F2, F10).

---

## Reported Clinical Phenotypes and Cohort Frequencies

Across published cohorts, cognitive impairment and severe expressive language delays represent the most penetrant findings, accompanied by variable neuromuscular, behavioral, and minor structural anomalies (F1, F2, F3, F6).

## Discriminating Features and Differential Diagnosis

### Core Discriminating Profile

When evaluating a patient with developmental delay, the clinical triad most indicative of *FBXO11* involves:
1. **Severe expressive language delay out of proportion to motor delay**, seen in 95-100% of index cases (14/14 in Gregor et al. 2018; 20/21 in Goel et al. 2026), where children exhibit absent or severely limited speech (F3, F18).
2. **Infantile/childhood axial hypotonia** (~62% penetrance in Goel et al. 2026 and Jansen et al. 2019) (F1, F17).
3. **Externalizing behavioral dysregulation**, comprising hyperactivity, ADHD, impulsivity, or aggressive outbursts (~62-85% in Western cohorts) (F1, F6, F11).

### Differential Diagnosis and Discrimination Confidence

* **Cornelia de Lange Syndrome (CdLS; *NIPBL*, *SMC1A*, *HDAC8*)**:
  * *Distinguishing Features*: CdLS typically exhibits characteristic synophrys, severe congenital microcephaly, and major structural limb-reduction defects (HP:0002814) (F2).
  * *Discrimination Confidence*: **High**. Limb-reduction defects have 0% penetrance (0/21) in *FBXO11* cohorts, and severe congenital structural limb malformations reliably exclude *FBXO11* (F2, F4).
* **Rubinstein-Taybi Syndrome (RSTS; *CREBBP*, *EP300*)**:
  * *Distinguishing Features*: RSTS is characterized by broad, angulated thumbs and halluces, severe postnatal growth failure, and high rates of complex congenital cardiac disease (F2, F4).
  * *Discrimination Confidence*: **High**. Complex congenital heart defects (HP:0001627) and angulated broad digits are absent in *FBXO11* carriers (F2, F4).
* **Coffin-Siris Syndrome (CSS; *ARID1B*, *SMARCB1*, etc.)**:
  * *Distinguishing Features*: CSS features hypoplasia or aplasia of the fifth fingernails/toenails (HP:0001792/HP:0001800) and coarse facies with generalized hypertrichosis (F5, F15).
  * *Discrimination Confidence*: **Moderate to High**. While generalized hypertrichosis and absent fifth digits are not part of the *FBXO11* phenotype, caution is warranted regarding nail and nasal morphology: specific *FBXO11* variants can phenocopy Trichorhinophalangeal syndrome (TRPS)-like phenotypes including nail hypoplasia (HP:0001792) and pear-shaped nose (HP:0000447) (Mégarbané et al., 2024, PMID: 38679370) (F5).
* **Koolen-de Vries Syndrome (KdVS; *KANSL1*)**:
  * *Distinguishing Features*: KdVS typically presents with a friendly, hyper-affable personality and distinctive pear-shaped nose (F15).
  * *Discrimination Confidence*: **Moderate**. Differentiating *FBXO11* from KdVS on dysmorphic grounds alone is challenging due to the nonspecific facial features of *FBXO11* patients; however, friendly demeanor contrasts with the disruptive, hyperactive, or aggressive behavioral abnormalities often observed in *FBXO11* (F6, F11, F15).

## Pertinent Negative Findings (Exclusionary Criteria)

In syndromic neurodevelopmental evaluations, negative discrimination often relies on the structural non-involvement of organ systems rather than cognitive hallmarks (F2). The presence of the following findings strongly argues **against** a diagnosis of *FBXO11*-related disorder:

1. **Congenital Limb-Reduction Deficiencies (HP:0002814)**: Completely absent across reported cohorts (0/21 in Goel et al.; 0/12 in Pan et al., 2024) (F2, F4). Presence of phocomelia, oligodactyly, or terminal limb deficiencies effectively excludes *FBXO11* (F2).
2. **Complex Structural Congenital Heart Defects (HP:0001627)**: Severe cyanotic heart disease, outflow tract defects, or heterotaxy are near-zero in penetrance (F2, F4). Only minor septal or transient findings have been reported (2/20 in Gregor et al. 2018; 4/21 in Goel et al. 2026; 0/12 in Pan et al. 2024) (F2, F4).
3. **Profound Congenital Microcephaly**: Persistent, profound primary microcephaly (head circumference < -3 to -4 SD at birth) is atypical; head circumferences typically fall within the normal range or show mild-to-moderate deviations (F4, F7, F9).
4. **Progressive Structural Neurodegeneration / Cerebral Atrophy**: Progressive motor loss, dementia, or active loss of milestones without epilepsy argues against IDDFBA, which characteristically presents as a static neurodevelopmental delay (F4, F8).

## Genotype-Phenotype, Age, and Sex Stratification

### Variant Type and Domain Position
* **Haploinsufficiency Convergence**: Pathogenic variants in *FBXO11* include de novo loss-of-function variants (frameshift, nonsense, canonical splice site mutations; 10/20 in Gregor et al. 2018) and missense variants (8/20 in Gregor et al. 2018) clustering in critical functional domains, specifically the F-box domain and UBR-like zinc finger domains (F1). Both mutational classes destabilize the SCF (SKP1-cullin-F-box) ubiquitin ligase complex or disrupt substrate interactions, converging on a single functional threshold deficit (F1). To date, no statistically distinct genotype-phenotype divergence separates loss-of-function from domain-specific missense alleles (F1).
* **Phenotypic Mimicry**: Rare specific missense variants can alter substrate interactions resulting in atypical phenocopies, such as the Trichorhinophalangeal syndrome-like presentation described by Mégarbané et al. (2024, PMID: 38679370) (F5).

### Sex Stratification
* There is no documented predilection for biological sex; males and females present with comparable frequencies, intellectual disability severity, and dysmorphic features across independent series (Gregor et al., 2018; Goel et al., 2026; Pan et al., 2024) (F1, F2, F3).

### Age-Dependent Evolution
* **Speech and Motor Milestones**: Developmental delays present in infancy with delayed motor milestones and marked expressive speech delay (F1, F3, F18).
* **Behavioral Dynamics**: Initial pediatric reports emphasized hyperactive and disruptive behaviors (ADHD, tantrums, aggressive outbursts) during early childhood (Gregor et al., 2018; Jansen et al., 2019) (F6, F11, F19). While hypotheses suggested an obligatory shift toward internalizing symptoms (anxiety, depression) in adulthood, cross-sectional and adult reports demonstrate that externalizing behaviors can persist into late adolescence (e.g., at 14 years; Fritzen et al., 2018, DOI: 10.1007/s00439-018-1892-1) and adulthood (Mainali et al., 2023, DOI: 10.1002/ajmg.a.63053), while internalizing symptoms frequently co-occur rather than replacing externalizing traits (F19).

## Contested and Contradictory Literature Findings

Evaluation of the literature highlights several deprioritized or contested hypotheses where cohorts exhibit conflicting phenotypic data:

### 1. Neurodevelopmental Regression
* **Deprioritized Claim**: *FBXO11*-related disorder is strictly static and never exhibits developmental regression (HP:0002376) (F8).
* **Contradictory Evidence**: Gregor et al. (2018, DOI: 10.1016/j.ajhg.2018.07.009) documented a patient experiencing developmental regression at age 3 years (F8). Goel et al. (2026) similarly noted regression of previously acquired language skills in 1 of 21 patients (F8). Furthermore, *FBXO11* is cataloged among developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) genes where seizures may induce skill loss (Hebbar & Mefford, 2020, PMC7076331) (F8). Thus, while non-progressive static delay is the norm, the absolute exclusion of regression is refuted (F8).

### 2. Microcephaly vs. Macrocephaly
* **Deprioritized Claim**: Microcephaly is consistently absent, and head circumference is typically skewed toward macrocephaly (F4, F7, F9).
* **Contradictory Evidence**: Gregor et al. (2018) identified microcephaly in 4 of 19 patients (21%), and Goel et al. (2026) observed microcephaly in 24% (5/21) (F1, F4, F7, F9). Conversely, Lee et al. (2020, DOI: 10.1002/ajmg.a.61828) described an inherited familial cohort where all 3 affected individuals exhibited macrocephaly (3/3), while Gregor et al. (2018) observed macrocephaly in only 2 of 19 (F4, F9). Head circumference exhibits wide variability rather than unidirectional skewing (F7, F9).

### 3. Penetrance of Behavioral Abnormalities
* **Deprioritized Claim**: Hyperactive, disruptive behavioral abnormalities are an obligatory hallmark distinguishing *FBXO11* from quiet syndromic ID (F11).
* **Contradictory Evidence**: While Gregor et al. (2018) observed behavioral disturbances in 85% (17/20) and Goel et al. (2026) reported 62% (13/21), Pan et al. (2024, DOI: 10.1038/s10038-024-01255-4) documented 12 affected individuals across 5 Chinese families where disruptive behavioral problems were conspicuously absent (only 1/12 had psychiatric hallucinations) (F6, F11). Familial cases often present with mild ID without behavioral disturbances (Lee et al., 2020) (F11).

### 4. Obesity and Body Weight
* **Deprioritized Claim**: Obesity/increased body weight (HP:0001513) is present in >50% of patients and serves as a reliable discriminator against growth-failure syndromes (F12).
* **Contradictory Evidence**: While Pan et al. (2024) noted increased body weight in 58.3% (7/12), Gregor et al. (2018) observed obesity in only 25% (5/20), and Gregor et al. (2022, PMC8825234) found increased weight/hyperphagia in only 17.4% (4/23) (F12). Moreover, short stature occurred in 50% (6/12) of Pan et al.'s cohort and 20% (4/20) of Gregor et al.'s cohort, demonstrating that growth failure can co-occur (F12).

### 5. Seizure Frequency
* **Deprioritized Claim**: Seizures (HP:0001250) affect >40% of cases and serve as an active discriminator (F13, F16).
* **Contradictory Evidence**: Goel et al. (2026) reported seizures in 43% (9/21), but Gregor et al. (2018) found seizures in 25% (5/20) and Pan et al. (2024) in 25% (3/12) (F13, F16). Seizures are absent in 57-75% of individuals, precluding their utility as a primary discriminating feature (F16).

## Synthesis, Cross-Cutting Patterns, and Diagnostic Algorithm

### Clinical Diagnostic Heuristic

```
Patient with Global Developmental Delay / Intellectual Disability
  │
  ├── [Presence of Major Malformations?]
  │     ├── Complex CHD or Limb Reduction Defect ──> ARGUES AGAINST FBXO11 (Consider CdLS, RSTS)
  │     └── Intact Limb & Cardiac Anatomy ─────────> RETAIN FBXO11 ON DIFFERENTIAL
  │
  └── [Core Neurodevelopmental Profile]
        ├── Severe Expressive Language Delay (~95-100%)
        ├── Infantile / Axial Hypotonia (~60%)
        └── Externalizing Behavior / Hyperactivity (~60-85% in de novo cases)
              │
              └── Next-Generation Sequencing (Exome/Genome Panel) to confirm FBXO11 variant
```

### Key Conclusions
1. **Do not exclude based on absent behavior**: Titular behavioral problems exhibit incomplete penetrance, especially in familial cohorts (Pan et al., 2024) (F6, F11).
2. **Rely on organ sparing**: Exclusionary power stems from the near-total absence of major structural congenital malformations (heart defects, limb reductions) rather than pathognomonic facies (F2, F4).
3. **Expect non-specific dysmorphism**: Facies are minor and subtle in roughly one-third of individuals; molecular testing remains required for definitive diagnosis (Jansen et al., 2019; Goel et al., 2026) (F10, F14, F15).
