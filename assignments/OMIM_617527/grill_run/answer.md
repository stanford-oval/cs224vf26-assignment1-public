## 1. Executive Summary: Phenotypic Identification of PLAA Deficiency (NDMSBA)

Neurodevelopmental disorder with progressive microcephaly, spasticity, and brain anomalies (NDMSBA; OMIM #617527) is an ultra-rare autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disorder caused by biallelic mutations in the *PLAA* gene (chromosome 9p21) [F2]. Because initial neonatal clinical examinations and cranial biometrics are typically normal or near-normal, diagnosing PLAA deficiency on phenotypic grounds alone requires recognizing its pathognomonic longitudinal evolution: an infant born normocephalic who experiences early-infantile developmental stagnation and hypotonia, rapidly accelerating into severe postnatal progressive microcephaly, spastic quadriparesis, secondary callosal thinning, and diffuse cerebral/cerebellar volume loss with delayed myelination [F1, F4, F7, F9, F10]. Clinicians must distinguish this specific temporal trajectory of active neurodegenerative decay from static congenital malformations (such as primary microcephaly) and perinatal acquired brain injury [F5].

## 3. Key Discriminating Features and Differential Diagnosis

To identify an affected individual among patients with overlapping infantile neurodegenerative phenotypes, clinicians should focus on discriminating features ranked below by diagnostic utility and distinguishing confidence [F5, F7, F9].

### 1. Longitudinal Biometrics: Normocephaly at Birth Transitioning to Rapid Postnatal Microcephaly
* **Discriminating Power**: Extremely high [F5, F7, F10].
* **Phenotypic Manifestation**: Affected neonates typically display normal birth occipitofrontal circumference (OFC) (e.g., around the 50th centile), with severe deceleration beginning between 2 to 4 months of age and dropping precipitously to below -3 to -4 standard deviations [F5, F7, F10].
* **Differentiating Conditions**:
  * *Autosomal Recessive Primary Microcephaly (MCPH)*: Primary microcephalies present with marked congenital microcephaly (congenital OFC <-3 SD) evident at birth or on second-trimester ultrasound, exhibiting a static cranial trajectory without profound neurodegenerative regression [F5, F6]. **Diagnostic distinction confidence: High** [F5].
  * *Pontocerebellar Hypoplasia (PCH)*: Classic PCH subtypes (e.g., PCH2 due to *TSEN54*) can also show near-normal birth OFC with late prenatal or postnatal microcephalic drop [F6]. Thus, microcephalic kinetics alone cannot completely exclude PCH, although supratentorial callosal and white matter loss are far more severe in PLAA deficiency [F6, F17]. **Diagnostic distinction confidence: Moderate to High** [F6].

### 2. Neuroimaging Evolution: Progressive Secondary Callosal Thinning vs. Primary Agenesis
* **Discriminating Power**: High [F1, F9].
* **Phenotypic Manifestation**: On early neonatal neuroimaging, the corpus callosum may appear relatively normal; however, serial MRI reveals progressive thinning and volumetric hypoplasia of the corpus callosum (>90% penetrance) secondary to widespread cortical neuron and axonal degeneration [F1, F9].
* **Differentiating Conditions**:
  * *Congenital Corpus Callosum Dysgenesis / Primary Agenesis*: Lacks serial thinning kinetics and demonstrates Probst bundles or malformed callosal architecture from birth without progressive white matter loss [F1, F9]. **Diagnostic distinction confidence: High** [F9].

### 3. Neurodegeneration Mimicking Cerebral Palsy Without Hypoxic-Ischemic Encephalopathy (HIE)
* **Discriminating Power**: High [F4, F5, F7, F18].
* **Phenotypic Manifestation**: The rapid progression from infantile hypotonia to severe spastic quadriparesis, combined with secondary microcephaly, often leads to misclassification as bilateral spastic or dyskinetic cerebral palsy (CP) [F4, F18]. The hallmark discriminator is the absence of perinatal asphyxia, normal gestational/birth history, and the presence of progressive regression (loss of milestones, progressive cortical atrophy) rather than static neurological deficit [F4, F5, F7]. **Diagnostic distinction confidence: High** [F5, F18].

### 4. Overlap with PEHO Syndrome Spectrum
* **Discriminating Power**: Moderate [F8, F19].
* **Phenotypic Manifestation**: Features of progressive encephalopathy, optic atrophy, and intractable seizures or hypsarrhythmia frequently trigger evaluation for PEHO (Progressive Encephalopathy with edema, Hypsarrhythmia, and Optic atrophy) or PEHO-like syndromes [F8]. While *PLAA* is a proven cause of PEHO-like encephalopathy (accounting for 1/19 cases in targeted PEHO cohorts), classic peripheral subcutaneous edema of the hands and feet is typically absent or non-prominent in *PLAA* cohorts [F4, F8]. **Diagnostic distinction confidence: Moderate** [F8].

## 4. Findings Looked for and Found Absent (Exclusionary Criteria)

In postnatal cohorts, several clinical and systemic findings have been systematically evaluated and found absent; their presence strongly argues against isolated *PLAA* deficiency [F3, F13, F15]:

1. **Extraneural Structural Malformations in Postnatal Patients**:
   * Postnatal infants with *PLAA* variants consistently lack major non-CNS congenital malformations [F3, F13]. Congenital heart defects, renal agenesis/dysplasia, and limb reduction defects have an evaluated frequency of <5% (0/7 in Falik Zaccai et al.; 0/7 in Hall et al.) [F3, F13]. The presence of complex congenital heart disease or visceral organ malformations strongly points to alternative syndromic diagnoses [F3, F13].
2. **Biochemical and Storage Disease Markers**:
   * Extensive metabolic profiling—including serum amino acids, urine organic acids, lysosomal enzymes, and standard endosomal/lysosomal storage biomarkers—has been tested and found consistently negative/normal in classic *PLAA* cohorts, excluding classic mucopolysaccharidoses, sphingolipidoses, and peroxisomal disorders [F15].
3. **Classic "Dragonfly" Cerebellar Hypoplasia Pattern**:
   * While cerebellar hemispheric and vermian volume loss is common on serial neuroimaging, the classic TSEN-associated dragonfly pattern (severe flattening of cerebellar hemispheres with preserved vermis) is absent on early neonatal MRI [F15, F17].

## 5. Phenotypic Modifiers: Variant Type, Position, Age, and Sex

### Sex Predilection
* The disorder is inherited in an autosomal recessive fashion (*PLAA* resides on chromosome 9p21.3) [F2]. Across published discovery and validation cohorts (e.g., Falik Zaccai et al., Hall et al., Han et al., Chitre et al.), affected males and females are equally represented, with no evidence of sex-influenced penetrance, symptom severity, or clinical divergence [F2].

### Age and Temporal Kinetics
* Age serves as the primary phenotypic determinant in *PLAA* deficiency [F1, F5, F7]. Clinical and radiological findings undergo an obligate temporal sequence:
  * *0–2 months*: Normal or near-normal head circumference, mild non-specific axial hypotonia, and relatively preserved neuroimaging [F5, F7, F10].
  * *2–6 months*: Arrest of motor milestone acquisition, abrupt deceleration of cranial growth resulting in progressive microcephaly, emergence of pyramidal tract signs, and initial corpus callosum thinning [F4, F5, F7, F9, F10].
  * *6–24 months*: Fully established spastic quadriparesis, severe secondary microcephaly (often <-4 SD), optic atrophy, onset of epileptic seizures, delayed or defective CNS myelination, and profound cerebral atrophy [F1, F4, F7, F8, F9].

### Variant Type and Position
* Both homozygous missense variants (e.g., c.2254C>T [p.Leu752Phe], c.68G>T [p.Gly23Val]) and compound heterozygous loss-of-function (frameshift, nonsense, canonical splice site) mutations lead to severe neurodevelopmental arrest and leukoencephalopathy [F2, F4, F9, F16].
* Although functional studies indicate that complete knockout or severe loss-of-function disrupts ubiquitin-mediated endolysosomal degradation of synaptic vesicle proteins, a strict categorical genotype-phenotype correlation among postnatally surviving patients has not been demonstrated; both missense and truncating alleles produce a shared, severe, early-infantile neurodegenerative phenotype [F2, F4, F16].

## 6. Literature Contradictions and Deprioritised Hypotheses

Several hypotheses and phenotypic assertions across the literature have revealed direct contradictions or were deprioritised due to conflicting evidence:

### 1. Postnatal Isolated Neurodegeneration vs. Prenatal Lethal Dysmorphology (F12, F13, F16)
* **Contradiction**: Pediatric clinical literature (Falik Zaccai et al., 2017; Hall et al., 2017; Dai et al., 2019; Han et al., 2025) characterizes *PLAA* deficiency exclusively as a postnatal, organ-restricted neurodegenerative leukoencephalopathy with strictly normal extraneural organogenesis [F3, F13]. Conversely, a fetal whole-exome sequencing study in deceased fetuses (Huang et al., 2023) identified biallelic *PLAA* variants in fetuses exhibiting major ultrasound anomalies, including skeletal and genitourinary defects [F11, F12, F13].
* **Resolution & Deprioritisation**: Hypothesis [F12] (positing that extraneural fetal findings represent secondary recessive diagnoses in consanguineous families) and [F13]/[F16] (positing that complete LoF nullizygosity causes embryonic/fetal multiorgan malformations while missense causes isolated neurodegeneration) remain contested [F12, F13, F16]. Postnatally viable mutations like homozygous p.Leu752Phe act as functional loss-of-function alleles without causing extraneural defects, whereas murine knockout models develop craniofacial/CNS defects rather than widespread extraneural malformations [F16]. Until fetal autopsy cohorts rule out secondary co-inherited variants, extraneural anomalies cannot be considered established direct consequences of *PLAA* deficiency [F3, F12].

### 2. Temporal Kinetics of Infratentorial vs. Supratentorial Atrophy (F14, F17)
* **Contradiction**: Earlier studies suggested that progressive cerebral atrophy significantly precedes cerebellar atrophy, claiming that cerebellar volume loss is an obligate late finding appearing only after 12 months of age [F14, F17]. In contrast, Dehghani et al. (2021) and Chitre et al. (2018) documented prominent cerebellar vermian hypoplasia and cerebellar atrophy in infants as young as 14 days to 2.5 months of age, with some cases clinically categorized as pontocerebellar hypoplasia type 3 (PCH3) [F14, F17].
* **Deprioritisation Reason**: Hypotheses [F14] and [F17] were deprioritised because cerebellar involvement is neither strictly delayed nor secondary across all patients; infratentorial atrophy can emerge in the early neonatal period, directly overlapping with primary PCH variants [F14, F17].

### 3. Quantitative Prevalence in Atypical Cerebral Palsy (F18)
* **Refuted Finding**: Hypothesis [F18] asserted that *PLAA* variants account for 1–2% of atypical or genetic cerebral palsy cohorts [F18].
* **Refutation**: This claim was based on extrapolating from a single small cohort of 10 atypical CP patients (Han et al., 2025), where exactly 1 patient harbored a pathogenic *PLAA* variant (1/10 = 10%) [F18]. Extrapolating a fixed 1–2% prevalence across all CP mimics suffers from extreme sample ascertainment bias; in unselected CP populations, *PLAA* is an ultra-rare monogenic cause [F2, F18].

### 4. Serial MRI Differentiation from ZNHIT3-Associated PEHO Syndrome (F19)
* **Refuted Finding**: Hypothesis [F19] proposed that serial neuroimaging differentiates *PLAA* disorder from classical *ZNHIT3*-associated Finnish PEHO syndrome by showing an initially preserved cerebellum in *PLAA* versus congenital cerebellar hypoplasia at birth in *ZNHIT3* [F19].
* **Refutation**: Both premises are contradicted by clinical data: *PLAA* patients have documented cerebellar atrophy on MRI as early as 14 days of life, while classical *ZNHIT3*-associated PEHO syndrome is defined by rapid postnatal cerebellar granule cell neurodegeneration rather than congenital absence at birth [F19].

## 7. Cross-Cutting Diagnostic Patterns and Synthesis

Integrating the clinical, radiological, and genetic evidence reveals three overarching diagnostic patterns that empower a clinician to identify *PLAA* deficiency from phenotypes alone:

1. **The Dynamic Neurodegenerative Trajectory vs. Static Congenital Malformation**:
   * The single most powerful clinical diagnostic indicator is the temporal arc: an infant who is structurally and biometrically normal at birth but demonstrates developmental arrest, rapid drop in head circumference centiles, and progressive secondary thinning of the corpus callosum and cerebral cortex between 2 and 6 months of age [F1, F5, F7, F9, F10].
2. **The Endolysosomal Trafficking Phenocopy Cluster**:
   * *PLAA* functions in ubiquitin-mediated endolysosomal degradation of synaptic membrane proteins [F1, F4]. Its disruption produces a phenotypic spectrum that bridges atypical progressive cerebral palsy, PEHO-like encephalopathy, and pontocerebellar hypoplasia [F4, F8, F14, F18]. When evaluating an infant diagnosed with "atypical spastic CP" lacking perinatal asphyxia, or a patient suspected of PEHO syndrome who lacks severe peripheral edema, *PLAA* should be prioritized on targeted gene panels [F4, F8, F18].
3. **Exclusion via Non-CNS Congenital Anomalies**:
   * In living infants presenting with microcephaly and spasticity, the presence of major extraneural structural defects (cardiac, renal, limb reduction) serves as an exclusionary criterion, pointing away from isolated *PLAA* deficiency toward broader syndromic or chromosomal etiologies [F3, F13].
