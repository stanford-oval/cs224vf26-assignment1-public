# Phenotypic Identification and Clinical Differentiation of Jaberi-Elahi Syndrome (GTPBP2; OMIM #617988)

## Introduction and Diagnostic Context

Jaberi-Elahi syndrome (JABELS, OMIM #617988) is an ultra-rare autosomal recessive neurodevelopmental and neuroectodermal disorder caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in *GTPBP2*, which encodes a GTP-binding protein essential for translational fidelity and ribosome quality control (Carter et al., 2019, DOI: 10.1111/cge.13523; Salpietro et al., 2024, DOI: 10.1016/j.ajhg.2023.11.012) [F1, F5]. Clinically, JABELS presents with severe developmental delay, progressive cerebellar atrophy, tone disturbances, microcephaly, and distinctive ectodermal anomalies (Manoochehri et al., 2024, PMID: 38852771; Soğukpınar et al., 2026, DOI: 10.1159/000552675/949444) [F1, F2]. Because core features heavily overlap with classic childhood neurodegenerative and neurodevelopmental conditions, identifying affected individuals based solely on phenotype requires precise knowledge of penetrance, age-dependent manifestation, and extraneurological discriminators.

## Discriminating Features and Differential Diagnosis

When evaluating a patient with severe early-onset neurodevelopmental delay, cerebellar atrophy, and axial hypotonia transitioning to limb spasticity, clinical differentiation is challenging. The conditions most commonly confused with JABELS are delineated below:

### 1. Infantile Neuroaxonal Dystrophy (INAD / PLAN; *PLA2G6*)
- **Overlapping Features**: Both conditions feature axial hypotonia progressing to spastic tetraparesis, cerebellar atrophy, optic atrophy, and corpus callosum thinning (Schneider, 2025; Gregory et al., 2025) [F12].
- **Discriminating Findings**: Ectodermal manifestations—specifically **sparse, slow-growing hair / alopecia**—occur in over 92% of JABELS patients (26/28) (Manoochehri et al., 2024; Carter et al., 2019) [F1, F2, F5], whereas hair development is typically normal in *PLA2G6*-related INAD. Furthermore, deep gray matter T2-hypointensity (iron deposition) is absent in infants with JABELS, appearing only in adult patients (ages 19–30 years) (Hajati et al., 2022) [F3].
- **Distinction Confidence**: **High** in children older than 12 months (due to progressive hypotrichosis); **Moderate to Low** in infants under 12 months who have not yet manifested ectodermal anomalies (Carter et al., 2019; Soğukpınar et al., 2026) [F2, F9, F14].

### 2. Hereditary Spastic Paraplegias (HSPs; Uncomplicated forms)
- **Overlapping Features**: Progressive lower limb spasticity, hyperreflexia, and motor delay [F1, F6, F11].
- **Discriminating Findings**: JABELS patients frequently exhibit early central hypotonia, microcephaly, **epilepsy, choreoathetosis**, severe global cognitive impairment (23/28), and ectodermal involvement, which are atypical for uncomplicated HSPs (Salpietro et al., 2024; Vedula et al., 2026) [F4, F6].
- **Distinction Confidence**: **High**.

### 3. Pontocerebellar Hypoplasias (PCH; e.g., *CASK*, *TSEN* complexes)
- **Overlapping Features**: Severe microcephaly, severe global developmental delay, and marked infratentorial volume loss [F4, F11, F12].
- **Discriminating Findings**: PCH classically demonstrates profound congenital pontine flattening and hypoplasia on MRI. In contrast, JABELS demonstrates primarily progressive cerebellar hemisphere and vermian atrophy with relatively preserved pontine bulk, paired with sparse scalp hair and late-onset retinal dystrophy (18/28) (Carter et al., 2019; Manoochehri et al., 2024) [F4, F8, F12].
- **Distinction Confidence**: **Moderate to High**.

### 4. Joubert Syndrome and Related Ciliopathies
- **Overlapping Features**: Hypotonia, cerebellar ataxia, developmental delay, and vermian hypoplasia [F12, F15].
- **Discriminating Findings**: False-positive MRI interpretations of severe cerebellar hypoplasia as a "molar tooth sign" (MTS) have led to misclassification of JABELS patients (D'Abrusco et al., 2022, DOI: 10.1007/s12311-021-01350-8) [F15]. True molar tooth sign morphology (elongated, thickened, horizontally oriented superior cerebellar peduncles) is absent in JABELS upon careful neuroradiological review. JABELS patients also lack ciliary hallmarks (cystic renal disease, polydactyly, retinal coloboma) and instead exhibit prominent hypotrichosis [F10, F15].
- **Distinction Confidence**: **Moderate** (relies on expert neuroradiological evaluation and trichoscopy) [F15].

## Features Absent or Incompatible (Arguing Against JABELS)

Identifying which findings argue against Jaberi-Elahi syndrome is critical for clinical rule-out:

1. **Primary Lethal Visceral Malformations and Dysplasias**: Complex congenital heart disease (e.g., conotruncal defects, heterotaxy), renal agenesis, cystic kidney disease, and primary skeletal dysplasias (e.g., osteochondrodysplasia) are not features of JABELS (Manoochehri et al., 2024; Carter et al., 2019) [F10]. *Clinical caveat*: Minor septal defects (e.g., an isolated secundum atrial septal defect) have been documented in a single Turkish infant (Soğukpınar et al., 2026), demonstrating that generic isolated atrial communication does not entirely exclude JABELS [F10].
2. **Primary Hepatic Failure, Renal Failure, or Corneal Clouding**: Hepatosplenomegaly, progressive hepatic cirrhosis, nephronophthisis, or corneal opacities point to lysosomal storage disorders, mitochondrial hepatopathies, or ciliopathies rather than JABELS [F10].
3. **Obligate Early Brain Iron Accumulation**: Basal ganglia T2-hypointensity/susceptibility artifact is absent in infancy and childhood in *GTPBP2* deficiency (only 4/28 total patients exhibited iron accumulation, all aged 19 to 30 years) (Hajati et al., 2022; Carter et al., 2019) [F1, F3]. Hence, requiring iron deposition on infant MRI will erroneously rule out JABELS.
4. **Normal Hair Growth Beyond 1–2 Years**: Because sparse hair is documented in >92% (26/28) of individuals, an older child with completely thick, robust scalp hair and normal density significantly diminishes the pre-test probability of JABELS [F2, F5].

## Genotype-Phenotype Correlations and Demographic Modifiers

### Variant Type and Position
- **Truncating vs. Missense Alleles**: The initial cohort descriptions highlighted biallelic truncating loss-of-function variants (nonsense, frameshift, splice-site) that resulted in a severe multisystem syndrome with marked skeletal deformities (kyphoscoliosis, equinovarus, contractures present in 23/28) (Jaberi et al., 2016; Carter et al., 2019) [F7, F10]. However, pathogenic missense variants (e.g., c.1289T>C [p.Leu430Pro] reported by Manoochehri et al., 2024, and homozygous missense variants in exon 7 described by Vedula et al., 2026) have been identified [F7, F16]. Missense variants can present with severe neurodevelopmental delay and seizures while sparing individuals from overt secondary skeletal deformities (0/1 proband affected in specific missense kindreds) (Manoochehri et al., 2024; Bertoli, 2018) [F7].
- **Corpus Callosum Dysgenesis**: Structural anomalies of the corpus callosum (thinning or hypogenesis) occur in only a subset of patients across variants and positions; its normal presence does not rule out JABELS (Bertoli, 2018; Manoochehri et al., 2024) [F4].

### Age-Dependent Evolution
- **Ectodermal Findings**: Hypotrichosis, dental delays, and nail abnormalities are not always present congenitally. Molecularly confirmed infants evaluated at 4 months of age exhibited normal hair without discernible ectodermal dysplasia (Soğukpınar et al., 2026; Carter et al., 2019) [F2, F9, F14]. Ectodermal signs emerge clearly between 1 and 3 years of life.
- **Neurodegeneration and Iron Accumulation**: Progressive cerebellar volume loss advances over time. Basal ganglia iron deposition is strictly an adult finding (documented exclusively in patients aged 19–30 years, 4/28) (Hajati et al., 2022) [F3].
- **Visual Impairment**: Retinal dysfunction and optic atrophy progress over childhood and adolescence, reported overall in 18/28 (64.3%) patients (Manoochehri et al., 2024) [F4, F8].

### Sex Modifiers
- Across reported cohorts, JABELS affects males and females equally, with no observed differences in clinical severity, progression, or organ penetrance attributable to biological sex [F16].

## Contradictions and Literature Discordances (Deprioritized Hypotheses)

Several concepts in early reports have been contested or refuted as larger literature series emerged:

1. **Mutational Homogeneity (Refuted)**: Early reports hypothesized that JABELS is caused solely by biallelic null truncating/splice mutations resulting in a completely uniform presentation (Carter et al., 2019) [F16]. This has been refuted by the identification of at least 5 pathogenic missense variants among 20 distinct *GTPBP2* variants (e.g., Manoochehri et al., 2024; Soğukpınar et al., 2026; Vedula et al., 2026), which introduce phenotypic pleiotropy (such as absent skeletal contractures) [F7, F16].
2. **Brain Iron Accumulation as a Universal Hallmark (Contested)**: Described initially as a core neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation (NBIA) syndrome (Jaberi et al., 2016), subsequent series proved that deep brain iron deposition occurs in only 4/28 individuals, restricted to adults aged 19–30 (Manoochehri et al., 2024; Hajati et al., 2022) [F1, F3]. Iron accumulation is an inconsistent, late-stage marker rather than an early obligate diagnostic criteria.
3. **Mandatory Ectodermal Dysplasia in Infancy (Contested)**: Multiple studies highlighted ectodermal signs as indispensable for clinical distinction from *PLA2G6*/INAD (Carter et al., 2019) [F12, F14]. However, a molecularly confirmed 4-month-old infant completely lacked ectodermal anomalies (Soğukpınar et al., 2026), proving that ectodermal hallmarks cannot serve as mandatory criteria in early infancy [F9, F14].
4. **Universal Visual Impairment and Optic Atrophy (Contested)**: While Jaberi et al. (2016) reported optic atrophy and retinal dysfunction in 3/3 affected siblings [F8, F11], systematic review across 28 individuals demonstrates penetrance of only 64.3% (18/28) (Manoochehri et al., 2024), indicating ophthalmologic involvement is variable and age-dependent [F4, F8].
5. **High Pretest Frequency of Joubert Syndrome Misdiagnosis (Contested)**: D'Abrusco et al. (2022) demonstrated that JABELS cerebellar hypoplasia could be misinterpreted as a molar tooth sign in Joubert-referral cohorts [F15]. However, systematic cohort reviews (Manoochehri et al., 2024) indicate this represents an isolated neuroimaging misinterpretation rather than a widespread feature across the JABELS literature [F15].

--- 

## Synthesis and Open Diagnostic Questions

1. **Age-Stratified Diagnostic Scoring**: Because canonical hallmarks (sparse hair, retinal dystrophy, deep brain iron accumulation) are age-dependent, clinical diagnostic algorithms must not penalize infants under 12 months for lacking ectodermal or neuroimaging changes [F1, F2, F9]. 
2. **Early Trichoscopic Screening**: While macroscopically visible alopecia develops past 1 year of age, systematic microscopic or dermoscopic examination of hair bulb density and hair shaft caliber in young infants undergoing workups for unexplained hypotonia or cerebellar volume loss may provide earlier discrimination from pure neurological phenocopies like *PLA2G6* [F2, F12, F14].
3. **Allelic Delineation**: Further functional and structural studies are needed to determine whether missense variants in specific *GTPBP2* domains retain partial translational elongation activity, explaining why some individuals avoid the severe musculoskeletal contractures and spine deformities observed in complete null states [F7, F16].
